# Discovery: investing, banking, lending

Written 2026-09-02. The last three. Same rule as the four before them:
every question traces to a regulator, to that regulator's own examination
findings, or to a published figure, and the discovery stays deterministic
because a suitability file has to be re-performable by a third party.

These three are different from the first four in one way worth naming.
For mortgage, auto, real estate and insurance, the regulator tells you
what the professional must **do**. For these three, the regulators have
also published, in numbers, what professionals were **found not doing**.
That is a better source than any survey, because it is the gap measured
rather than the gap reported, and it turns directly into questions a
person can ask.

---

## 1. Investing

### The regulator

The **CSA** and **CIRO**, under the **Client Focused Reforms**, in force
since 31 December 2021. In December 2025 they published what they found
after reviewing **105 registered firms**.

### The finding that reorders everything

Risk is **two things**, not one, and almost nobody outside the industry
knows this:

- **Risk tolerance** is how much loss you are *willing* to accept.
- **Risk capacity** is how much loss you can *afford* to absorb.

Your risk profile is supposed to be **the lower of the two**.

The single most common deficiency found across 105 firms was firms
continuing to rely on risk tolerance alone, or blending both into one
question that cannot be answered honestly. The reviewers gave an example
of the bad question:

> "I am prepared to assume a high level of investment risk and volatile
> fluctuations and am able to withstand losses"

A person with a high tolerance and a low capacity cannot answer that.
The two halves are opposite for them, and the form has no box for it.

So the question 4orm asks is the two-factor question, split, in plain
words: **which is smaller for you, what you are willing to lose or what
you could actually afford to lose?** Nobody is ever asked this, and it
is the answer that is supposed to constrain everything recommended.

### The other findings, and what each becomes

| What was found missing | The question it becomes |
|---|---|
| **Liquidity needs not collected**, a particular concern with illiquid securities | When would you need this money back? |
| **External holdings not collected**, so concentration cannot be assessed. Worse for exempt market dealers | Does whoever is advising you know what you hold elsewhere? |
| **Net asset ranges too broad** to be usable, e.g. "$1 million to $5 million" | Feeds the same question |
| **Spousal data combined**, preventing an individual suitability determination | Is any of this held jointly? |
| **KYC not updated** at the required 12 or 36 months, and life changes such as job loss, retirement or divorce not triggering an update | Anything changed since you last filled in a form for them? |
| **"Suitable" written with no supporting rationale** | Ask for the reason, in writing |
| **Cost impact on returns not considered** | Ask what it costs you a year, in dollars |
| **A reasonable range of alternatives not considered** | Ask what else was considered, and why not that |
| **Continuing to hold not treated as a recommendation** requiring its own suitability determination | Applies to money you already have with them |

### The question set

| # | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| V1 | What is happening | Being recommended something, moving an account, starting out, or a document in front of you |
| V2 | When would you need this money back | Liquidity needs and time horizon, both required KYC elements, both found missing |
| V3 | The nosy one: which is smaller, what you are willing to lose or what you could afford to lose | The two-factor risk profile. The most prevalent deficiency across 105 firms |
| V4 | Does whoever is advising you know what you hold elsewhere | Concentration. Cannot be assessed without it, and it was routinely not collected |
| V5 | What matters most | Growth, income, not losing it, or being able to reach it. The investment objective, in the person's own words rather than a box |

## 2. Banking

### The regulator

**FCAC**, and since 30 June 2022 the **Financial Consumer Protection
Framework** in the Bank Act.

### What FCAC measured

Mystery shopping across the domestic retail banks produced numbers that
are hard to argue with:

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| Premium credit card recommended **without asking about income** | **80%** |
| Any credit card recommended without asking about income | **79%** |
| Employees who asked about **spending habits** before recommending a premium card | **16%** |
| Chequing account recommendations judged **inappropriate** | **15%** |
| Credit card recommendations judged **inappropriate** | **20%** |
| **Cross-sold** products judged inappropriate | **20%** |
| Chequing interactions not "clear and simple" | **11%** |
| Credit card interactions unclear or overly complex | **17%** |
| Chequing interactions containing misleading information | **6%** |

A recommendation made without asking what you earn or what you spend is
not a recommendation. That is the whole basis of the second question.

### What the framework now gives a person

- Banks must **offer and sell products appropriate for the person's
  needs**, having collected and assessed their circumstances.
- **Express consent**, in writing or verbally, before anything is
  provided. Each optional product needs its **own separate agreement**;
  they cannot be bundled into one.
- **Refunds** of fees that were not disclosed, were disclosed
  incorrectly, or were charged for something never expressly consented
  to, plus interest.
- **Alerts** when a balance falls below $100, or a figure you set.
- **Renewal reminders** at 21 days and 5 days for terms of 30 days or
  more.
- **56 days** for the bank to deal with a complaint, then **OBSI**, which
  has 120 days to make a final recommendation.
- Government cheques cashed free up to **$1,750**.

### The question set

| # | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| B1 | What are you there for | Opening something, being offered something extra, reviewing what you pay, or a document in front of you |
| B2 | Did they ask what you need it for, before naming a product | The 79% and 80%. This is the appropriateness obligation, checked from the other side |
| B3 | What came with it | Creditor insurance, overdraft, a card, a line. The 20% cross-sell finding, and each one needs its own agreement and its own consent |
| B4 | The nosy one: do you know what this costs you a month, all in | Fees not disclosed are refundable, but only if somebody notices |
| B5 | What matters most | Lowest fees, being able to walk in somewhere, one place for everything, or building a credit history |

## 3. Lending

### The regulator

Federally, the **Criminal Code** rate cap and the **cost of borrowing
disclosure** rules; provincially, the cost of credit disclosure regimes
harmonised across the country; and **FCAC** for what actually happens to
borrowers.

### The rate cap changed, and most people have not heard

Since **1 January 2025** the criminal rate of interest is an **APR above
35%**, down from roughly 48%. Payday lending, where a province has its
own regime, sits outside that and is capped at **$14 per $100 borrowed**,
with a maximum $20 fee for a dishonoured payment.

That gives a person a fixed reference point they can hold against any
offer, which is rare in consumer finance.

### What must be in writing before anything is signed

A written **initial disclosure statement**, before the agreement is
signed, carrying:

- the **APR**
- the **total cost of credit**, in dollars
- the term and the amortisation
- the full payment schedule, amounts and dates
- **default charges**
- **prepayment rights**
- any optional or mandatory charge beyond interest

For a mortgage, that statement has to arrive **two business days before**
any obligation or payment, which is a cooling-off period most people do
not know they have.

### What FCAC found happens to borrowers

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| Borrowed for an **unexpected** necessary expense | 45% |
| Borrowed for an **expected** necessary expense, such as rent or a utility bill | 41% |
| Borrowed to avoid a late charge on a bill | 17% |
| Took **only one** loan in three years | 29% |
| Took **six or more** | 23% |
| Understood a payday loan costs more than the alternatives available to them | **43%** |
| Cost of $300 for fourteen days, payday versus a credit card advance | **$63 against $7.42** |

The 43% is the one that matters. The majority of people using the most
expensive credit in the country did not know it was the most expensive,
which means the useful thing to do is not to warn them off it but to
show them the comparison they were never shown.

The repeat-borrowing figures decide the fourth question. Somebody on
their fourth loan this year is in a different situation from somebody on
their first, and asking is the only way to know.

### The question set

| # | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | What is the money for | 45% unexpected, 41% expected. Naming it removes the shame, because most borrowing is for ordinary bills |
| L2 | Have you been given the rate and the total in dollars | The initial disclosure statement, which must exist before signing |
| L3 | Is anything else being added to it | Optional insurance and fees, both of which must be disclosed and are financed at the same rate |
| L4 | The nosy one: is this the first one this year | 23% took six or more in three years. It is the difference between a gap and a treadmill |
| L5 | What matters most | Smallest payment, paid off fastest, least total cost, or approval at all. The last is a real answer and gets a real reply |

---

## What none of these do

None of them says which fund, which bank, or whether to take the loan.
No page names a real firm as a bad actor. The rate cap is stated as a
fact about the law, never as an accusation about anybody's offer. Each
produces the same artefact the other four produce: the person's own
situation, in their own words, written down before anybody is selling,
plus a short list of things they can go and find out on their own.

The four excluded elicitation techniques stay excluded: no flattery to
deflect, no complaint baiting, no provocative statements, no presumptive
statements.
