Why it is hard

You Are Typing The Same Number Into Four Different Places.

A spreadsheet for the balances. A second spreadsheet because the first one got too big. The bank portal in one tab and the accounting system in another. A folder of signed approvals. An email thread where somebody explained the difference last March. None of it talks to any of the rest, and at month end you are the thing joining it together. We built this company because of that.

The part nobody puts in a brochure

The work is not hard. It is the same work, over and over, in six places.

If any of this is familiar, you are the person this company was built for.

The spreadsheet

And then the second one

One file held the balances until it got slow, so now there are two, and a third that somebody built to check the first two against each other.

The accounts

More than one bank

Different banks for different purposes, different portals, different export formats, different cut off times. Each one has to be pulled separately.

The ledgers

Two systems, one truth

The accounting system says one thing. The operational system says another. Neither is wrong. They were just built to answer different questions.

The same number

Entered three or four times

Once where it happened, once in the ledger, once in the spreadsheet, once in the report. Every retyping is a chance to be out by a digit.

The approvals

Sitting in email

Somebody signed off on the difference. The evidence that they did is in an inbox, and the inbox belongs to a person, not to the firm.

The month end

Two days you cannot plan around

It lands whether or not anything else is happening, and the person who does it is the only person who knows how it fits together.

Here is the thing that gets said on every call. I do not want another system. I want one place that tells me what I need to do and shows me it is done. That sentence is the entire product brief. Everything else on this site is detail underneath it.

Four firms

None of these firms is doing anything wrong. All four have the same problem.

These are composites, drawn to show the shape of the work rather than to describe any real business.

A payroll company

Priya runs payroll for 400 small businesses.

Money lands from each employer a few days before payday and sits with her firm until it reaches the staff. It is never her money. At the end of every single day she has to be able to say how much of the balance belongs to which employer.

What it feels like: the number is easy on a quiet Tuesday. It is hard on the Friday before a long weekend, when four employers funded late and one payment bounced back.

A law firm

Daniel has eleven lawyers and one bookkeeper.

House deposits, settlement money and retainers sit in the firm's trust account. Every month the bookkeeper checks the bank against the firm's own list, client by client, and the report has to be finished inside a window set by the law society.

What it feels like: a few days of month end every month, and a partner signing something they did not personally check because they have to trust the bookkeeper.

An insolvency trustee

Amara administers three hundred estates.

Each bankruptcy is its own pot of money owed to the people waiting to be paid. Every one of those accounts has to be reconciled every month, within forty five days of the bank statement date, under a federal directive.

What it feels like: three hundred small reconciliations rather than one big one, every month, forever, and each one is somebody's money.

A property manager

Ben manages twelve hundred rental units.

Rent belongs to landlords. Damage deposits belong to tenants and have to come back. Both sit in trust, tracked unit by unit, reconciled monthly to a provincial council that can ask for the working at any point.

What it feels like: a spreadsheet beside the accounting system, and a quiet worry about what happens if the person who maintains it leaves.

What actually goes wrong

Five failures, and not one of them is laziness.

1

The record is in six places

A bank statement, two spreadsheets, the accounting system, a folder of signed approvals, an email chain. Nothing joins them, so the proof gets assembled by hand every single time somebody asks.

2

The deadline does not move

Monthly, or forty five days, or every single day. It arrives whether or not the person who does it is available.

3

Problems are found late

A difference between two records is usually noticed during the check, not on the day it happened, which is when it would have been easy to explain.

4

The rule changes underneath

A record made in 2026 was made under the rule as it stood in 2026. Almost nobody keeps a copy of the wording that applied at the time.

5

Producing it takes weeks

A request for a period years back turns into a search across systems, filing cabinets and people who may have left the firm.

Notice what all five have in common. They are failures of record keeping, not failures of honesty. The money is usually exactly where it should be. What is missing is the ability to show it, quickly, for a day that has already passed.

Where the opening is

A new duty, a published list of who owes it, and no tool built for it.

This is the part that made us change what we were building.

01

The duty is already live

There is no bill still to pass and no group of firms that has to agree first. Supervision of payment firms started on 8 September 2025 and the rules are being checked now.

02

The buyers are on a public list

Firms that register are named publicly. The Bank of Canada reported about 1,600 payment providers registered in Canada in 2025. You do not have to guess who owes the duty.

03

A daily duty builds a habit

A yearly duty buys one order from a supplier. A daily one becomes part of how the firm works, and that is what lasts when a competitor turns up.

04

A dated review is waiting

Every registered firm owes an independent review by 8 September 2028, and that review reads the three years before it. The records being made today are the ones it will read.

05

The work is being done by hand

Spreadsheets beside accounting systems, month end scrambles, folders of signed paper. The general purpose tools were not built for this and the specialist tools were built for other countries.

06

The same job, ten times over

Ten kinds of firm, ten regulators, ten deadlines, and very nearly one shape of work underneath all of them.

We are not claiming nobody has thought of this. We are saying the Canadian version of it does not exist yet. The rules are Canadian, the deadlines are Canadian, the professional bodies are provincial, and software built for another country's rulebook does not simply cross the border.
